An accounting firm's phone has two modes. From January through mid-April it rings constantly: new clients shopping for a preparer, existing clients asking what to bring, everyone with a deadline and a question. From May through November it's quiet enough that you wonder why you pay for the line. An AI receptionist for accountants exists for exactly this shape of demand, because software scales with the season and payroll doesn't.

## Why tax season breaks the normal staffing math

Most service businesses have busy days. Accounting has a busy season, and that difference is what makes staffing so awkward.

If you hire a front-desk person for the January-through-April surge, you have three options, none of them great. Keep them year-round and pay full wages through months when the phone barely rings. Hire seasonally and spend every December recruiting and training someone who leaves in May, then repeat next year. Or skip the hire and let calls roll to whoever can grab them, which in a small firm usually means a preparer stops billable work to recite the document checklist for the fourth time that day.

The timing makes it sharper. People shop for a new accountant in the exact months your line is busiest. A February caller who hits voicemail is often comparing three firms, and the firm that answers tends to get the consultation.

An answering service for tax firms is the traditional fix, and human services are genuinely good at warmth. But most bill by the minute or by the call, so your cost spikes exactly when your volume does. Software inverts that: one flat rate, multiple conversations at once, identical bill in April and July.

## What an AI receptionist actually handles for a firm

A good one works your real channels, phone, text, and website chat, and does the repetitive front-desk work completely.

| The caller | What the AI does |
|---|---|
| New client: "Do you take individual returns?" | Runs intake: filing status, personal or business, which deadline they're up against. Then books a consultation on your actual calendar and texts a confirmation. |
| "What do I need to bring?" | Reads back your document checklist, worded the same way every single time. |
| "What do you charge for a 1040? For a business return?" | Says exactly what you configured: a number, a range, or "it depends, and here's how we quote." |
| "Can I get in before the 15th?" | Offers only appointment slots that are genuinely open, mid-conversation. |
| 10:40 p.m. on April 12 | The same thing it does at 10:40 a.m. Answers, reassures, books the appointment. |

The consistency matters more than it sounds. A tired human on call forty of the day starts improvising and shortening. The AI gives answer four hundred exactly as it gave answer one. ## The hard boundary: it must never give tax advice

Read this section before you buy anything, from us or from anyone.

A receptionist, human or AI, answers questions about the *firm*. What do you charge, what should I bring, when can I come in, how does your process work. Those are facts you wrote down, and an AI can deliver them precisely.

Questions about the *caller's taxes* are a different category entirely. "Can I deduct my home office?" "Should we file jointly?" "Do I need to send my contractor a 1099?" A general-purpose chatbot will happily improvise answers to all of these, and improvised tax advice coming from your firm's phone number is a liability, not a receptionist.

The correct behavior is a polite, firm redirect: "That's exactly what your consultation is for. I can't advise on your taxes, but I can book you with someone who can. Does Tuesday at 2:00 work?" The caller gets what they actually need, which is time with a professional, and your firm never has an unlicensed algorithm dispensing opinions under its name.

## Confidentiality: what the front desk should never collect

Your firm handles some of the most sensitive data a client owns, and the receptionist layer shouldn't touch it. A well-scoped intake collects contact information and appointment context only: name, phone, email, what kind of return, what deadline. No Social Security numbers, no account numbers, no W-2 uploaded into a chat window. Sensitive documents flow through your firm's secure portal after the engagement starts, exactly as they do today. When you evaluate an AI receptionist for a CPA firm, ask what it is designed to collect. The right answer is enough to book the meeting, and not one field more.

## When you don't need this

Honesty costs us nothing here. A solo preparer with a stable book of returning clients and no interest in growing can thrive on voicemail and a good January email. If that's you, save the money.

If you already have a year-round admin who handles the phones well and you're not turning work away, keep them. This is not a replacement for a person who's already doing the job. ## FAQ

### Can an AI receptionist give tax advice?

No, and you should want it that way. Tax advice depends on a client's full situation and carries professional responsibility; a receptionist's job is to answer questions about your firm and get people booked. A properly configured AI answers "what do you charge" and "what do I bring" precisely, then redirects "can I deduct this" to a consultation. A vendor whose AI improvises answers to tax questions is selling you a liability.

### Can it handle new-client intake?

Yes, and it's arguably the AI's best job. It asks the questions you'd want asked every time: individual or business, filing status, which deadline they're facing, how they found you. Then it books the consultation on your real calendar and texts a confirmation. What it doesn't do is judge complexity or quote engagements that need a professional's eye. It gathers, books, and hands off.

### Is client information safe with an AI receptionist?

The honest answer is that safety comes from scope. The receptionist layer should collect contact details and appointment context only, never Social Security numbers, account numbers, or tax documents. Those belong in your firm's secure portal, where they already live. Ask any vendor exactly what their system collects and stores; a good one will have a short, specific answer.

### What happens during the April crush when call volume spikes?

This is where flat software pricing earns its keep. An AI receptionist handles simultaneous conversations, so ten callers at 9 a.m. on April 14 all get answered rather than queued. And because the price is a flat monthly rate, your busiest month costs the same as your quietest one. Per-minute services work the other way: April is precisely when their bill peaks.

### Can it schedule for multiple partners?

Velonyx books against the real Google Calendar you connect, so callers are only ever offered times that are genuinely open. If your partners keep separate calendars or you route certain client types to certain people, raise that in the setup conversation so the booking rules match how your firm actually runs. It's a configuration question worth asking any vendor before you sign.
